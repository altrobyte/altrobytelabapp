0110 min

What is actually moving

Every sensor reading, every motor, every Wi-Fi packet from an ESP32 — all of it is one thing underneath: electrons pushed through a material.

Copper atoms hold their outer electrons very loosely. Left alone, those free electrons wander randomly in every direction, so nothing useful happens. Connect a battery and the randomness gets a bias: now there is a slight, steady drift in one direction. That drift is current.

Random motion vs. drift

Same electrons, same wire. The only difference is whether a battery is connected.

Battery off — random motion
Charge (Q), measured in coulombs One coulomb is about 6.24 × 1018 electrons. You will never count them. You only care about how many pass a point per second.

One coulomb per second is one ampere. In embedded work you rarely see a full amp on a signal line — an ESP32 GPIO pin is happy around 10–20 mA, which is 0.010–0.020 coulombs every second. Chhota number, lekin poora system usi pe chalta hai.

Teaching cue — 10 min
  • Open with a burnt component or a dead battery in hand. Ask: "Why did this die?" Park the question, promise the answer by minute 60.
  • Run the drift demo twice. Point out that the electrons never speed up dramatically — the bias is what changes.
  • Do not define coulomb formally for more than 60 seconds. Move on.
0216 min

Voltage, current, resistance

Three quantities, one relationship. Get the intuition here and the maths for the rest of the course becomes bookkeeping.

Voltage (V), in volts The push. A difference in electrical pressure between two points. Voltage is always measured between two points — there is no such thing as the voltage "at" a pin without a reference, and that reference is usually ground.
Current (I), in amperes The flow. How much charge passes a point each second.
Resistance (R), in ohms The opposition. How hard the material fights that flow, turning the lost energy into heat.

Water tank ↔ circuit

Drag the two sliders. The tank and the circuit are the same system shown two ways.

Current10.0 mA
More pressure or a wider pipe both give more flow.

Where the analogy breaks

  • Water can spill out of an open pipe. Charge cannot — it must return to the source, so a circuit has to be a complete loop.
  • The tank empties. An ideal supply does not; a real battery does, and that is a separate topic called capacity.
  • Use the analogy to build intuition, then drop it. From Session 3 onward we speak only in volts and amps.
Teaching cue — 16 min
  • Ask a student to predict the current before you move a slider. Predict-then-check is what makes this stick.
  • Hammer one sentence: voltage is always between two points. Half of all beginner debugging failures come from ignoring this.
  • Show a multimeter on a live board here if you have one on the bench.

Reading a real resistor

You will pick up hundreds of these. The colour bands are the value — learn to read them and you stop guessing at the bench.

The first two bands are digits, the third is a multiplier, and the fourth is tolerance. Hold the resistor with the tolerance band (usually gold) on your right.

The three you will use constantly

  • 220 Ω / 330 Ω — in series with an LED, to limit current.
  • 4.7 kΩ / 10 kΩ — pull-up on I²C lines and on buttons, so an unconnected pin is never floating.
  • Two resistors as a divider — to bring a 12 V battery sense line down into the ESP32's 0–3.3 V ADC range. We build this in Session 4.
0416 min

Ohm's law

One line that ties the three quantities together. If you remember nothing else from today, remember this.

V = I × R   ·   I = V ÷ R   ·   R = V ÷ I

Solver and V–I graph

Enter any two values to get the third. The graph plots current against voltage — the slope is set by resistance.

Current = V ÷ R10.0 mA

Worked example — the LED on your board

A red LED drops about 2 V across itself and wants roughly 10 mA. Your ESP32 pin gives 3.3 V. How big should the series resistor be?

R = (3.3 V − 2.0 V) ÷ 0.010 A = 130 Ω

The nearest standard values are 150 Ω and 220 Ω. Pick 220 Ω: the LED is slightly dimmer, the pin is safer, and the part is in every kit. Jo milta hai wahi lagao, bas calculation ke upar wala value lo.

Teaching cue — 16 min
  • Make the room do the LED calculation on paper before you show the answer. Give them 3 minutes.
  • Then ask the follow-up: what happens with no resistor at all? Slide the resistance to minimum in the hero demo and let them see the current spike.
  • Flag that a diode is not ohmic — its 2 V drop is roughly fixed. That is why we subtract before dividing.
0514 min

Power and heat

Current through a resistance always produces heat. Every component you burn out on the bench is this equation being ignored.

P = V × I   =   I² × R   =   V² ÷ R

Power is measured in watts, which is joules per second — the rate at which electrical energy becomes something else. In a resistor that "something else" is entirely heat. In a motor most of it is motion. In an LED, light.

Burn a resistor (safely)

Choose a resistor's physical power rating, then push voltage until it can't cope.

Current50 mA
Power0.25 W

This is also why a battery-powered IoT node lives or dies on current. A node drawing 80 mA continuously from a 2000 mAh cell lasts about a day. The same node sleeping at 50 µA between readings lasts years. Same hardware, different power discipline.

0614 min

Capacitance

A capacitor stores charge and resists sudden changes in voltage. It is the component that keeps your microcontroller from resetting itself every time a motor kicks in.

Capacitance (C), in farads How much charge a component holds per volt applied. A farad is enormous; real parts are in microfarads (µF), nanofarads (nF) and picofarads (pF).

Two conductive plates separated by an insulator. Apply a voltage and charge piles up on the plates. Remove the source and the charge stays — the capacitor now behaves briefly like a small battery. It does not fill instantly, and how fast it fills depends on the resistance feeding it.

τ = R × C   ·   63% charged after 1τ  ·  ~99% after 5τ

Charging and discharging

Press charge. Change R or C and watch the curve get lazier or sharper.

Cap voltage0.00 V
A bigger R or a bigger C means a slower curve.

Where you will actually meet capacitors

PartJob on a real board
0.1 µF ceramicDecoupling. Sits right next to every IC's power pin and supplies the short, sharp current bursts the chip demands, so the supply rail doesn't dip.
10–470 µF electrolyticBulk storage near the power input. Smooths out the bigger, slower sags when a relay or motor switches.
Small cap + resistorDebouncing a mechanical button, and setting timing in RC circuits.

An electrolytic capacitor has polarity. Put it in backwards and it will fail — sometimes loudly. The stripe marks the negative leg.

078 min

Inductance

A capacitor resists a change in voltage. An inductor resists a change in current — and gets violent when you ignore it.

Inductance (L), in henries A coil of wire stores energy in a magnetic field. The field opposes any change in the current making it.

Switch an inductor on and current ramps up rather than jumping. Switch it off and the collapsing magnetic field tries to keep that current flowing — with nowhere to go, it produces a large reverse voltage spike. Any coil is an inductor: relays, solenoids, motor windings, buzzers.

The relay spike

Switch the coil on, let the current settle, then switch it off. Then try again with a flyback diode fitted.

With no diode, the collapsing field can produce well over a hundred volts across a transistor rated for 40.

This is the answer to the question from minute one. That is how components die: a relay driven directly by a transistor with no flyback diode across the coil. Fit the diode reversed across the coil and the current has a loop to die down in — the spike never forms. It costs two rupees and saves the board.

082 min

Quick check

Five questions. Answer honestly — this tells you whether to rewatch anything before Session 2.

Carry these five into next week

  • Voltage is a difference between two points. Always ask "relative to what?"
  • Current is what actually does the work, and what actually kills components.
  • V = IR is the whole first month of this course in three letters.
  • A capacitor smooths voltage. An inductor smooths current.
  • Anything with a coil in it needs a flyback diode.

Before Session 2

  • Read three resistors from your kit and write down their values.
  • Work out the series resistor for a green LED (2.1 V drop, 12 mA) on a 5 V rail.
  • Bring a multimeter if you own one. Session 2 is breadboard, ground, series and parallel, and your first measurement.
